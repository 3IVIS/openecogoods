A running shoe or sneaker is a bonded composite — foam midsole, rubber
outsole, textile or synthetic upper, all glued together — which is exactly
what [material honesty](/principles) warns against. That
construction is also why almost none of it gets recycled: separating the
layers isn't economical, so worn-out shoes overwhelmingly go to landfill or
incineration.

**Why it scores this way:** by far the largest unit volume on this
shortlist, but full open-source manufacture of a performance shoe (sole
molds, foam chemistry, lasts, multi-material bonding) is a genuinely hard
manufacturing problem — harder than furniture, on par with or past
textiles. The more tractable angle is probably component-level: resoling
kits, replaceable insoles, repair patches — a narrower slice of "shoes"
that's actually within reach of a small shop, rather than competing with
athletic-shoe factories on day one.

**By the numbers:** roughly 22–24 billion pairs of footwear are produced
globally each year, about 1.2 billion of them sneakers specifically —
comfortably the highest unit volume of anything on this shortlist. Running
shoes are typically rated for 300–500 miles, around four to six months of
regular use, and casual sneakers are commonly replaced every 6–12 months
regardless of visible damage.
